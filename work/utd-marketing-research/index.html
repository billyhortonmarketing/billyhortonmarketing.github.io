What could renew interest in Scouting?

A 25-response marketing research study explored perceptions of Scouting America, barriers to participation, and the kinds of programs that could renew interest.

THE CONTEXT

Start with
the people.

Scouting America has a recognizable name and a long association with outdoor learning. But recognition alone cannot explain why someone joins, stays involved, or chooses a different activity. For an independent UT Dallas Marketing Research class project—not a study commissioned by Scouting America—our five-person team explored how people perceive the organization and what could make its programs more appealing.

We distributed an anonymous Qualtrics survey through university and personal networks and collected approximately 25 responses. The questionnaire covered awareness, experience, perceived barriers, and interest in alternative program formats.

FROM FINDINGS TO QUESTIONS WORTH TESTING

The opportunity was not simply to remind people that Scouting exists. Respondents pointed toward a need for programs that feel manageable, worthwhile, and relevant to current family schedules. Outdoor skills still had appeal, while shorter formats and more flexible experiences looked like ideas worth testing.

TEAM RECOMMENDATIONS

Three directions
worth exploring.

01

Offer flexible ways to participate

Explore short outdoor experiences, seasonal participation, and event-based options that could better fit around sports and family schedules.

02

Strengthen the program experience

Standardize leader preparation, feedback channels, and accountability practices to address uneven experiences across troops.

03

Rebuild trust through clearer marketing

Showcase real experiences, safety practices, and skill-building outcomes through transparent communication and more relevant storytelling.

Methodology note: This was a small convenience sample of approximately 25 respondents, with many respondents outside the age range eligible for youth Scouting. Responses were self-reported, some answers were incomplete, and multiple selections were possible. The findings are directional, not representative of all families; the survey cannot establish actual membership drop-off or prove that a specific change would increase enrollment.
